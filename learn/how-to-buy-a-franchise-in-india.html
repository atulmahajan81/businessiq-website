How to Buy a Franchise in India

The complete process from first enquiry to opening day — what happens at each stage, what you sign, and where first-time investors most often go wrong.

Home › Learn › How to Buy a Franchise

Buying a franchise is less a purchase than a long commitment. You are not acquiring a business outright; you are licensing the right to operate one under someone else's brand and rules, usually for five to ten years. The process below reflects how that actually runs in India, including the steps brochures tend to skip.

Expect three to six months from first enquiry to opening. Lean formats move faster; full-format restaurants and larger retail take longer.

The nine steps

  1. Define your budget and your involvement

    Before looking at a single brand, settle two numbers and one decision. Your total available capital — including working capital, not just setup cost. Your realistic timeline. And whether you will run the outlet day to day or appoint a manager.

    That last decision narrows the field more than category preference does. An owner-operated café and an absentee-owned coaching centre are different businesses with different economics, whatever the brochures suggest.

  2. Shortlist brands against your profile

    Match brands to capital, city and involvement. When comparing, use total setup cost, never the franchise fee alone — the fee is typically a fraction of what you actually spend. Two brands quoting similar fees can differ by tens of lakhs once fit-out and equipment are counted.

  3. Register as an investor

    Registering with a consulting network unlocks detailed brand information, financials and introductions. A consultation fee applies, payable to Franchise India rather than to BusinessIQ. Establish exactly what the fee includes before paying it.

  4. Meet the brand and run due diligence

    This is the most informative step, and the one most often rushed. Attend brand meetings or a discovery day, request outlet-level financials, and ask how many franchisees have left in the last three years and why.

    Do the one thing most investors skip: speak to current and former franchisees, without the brand arranging the call. Former franchisees in particular will tell you things no brochure will. If a franchisor is reluctant to let you contact its network, treat that as information.

  5. Model the unit economics yourself

    Build your own profit and loss for a single outlet, using local rent and salary figures rather than the brand's national averages. Work out average transaction value, realistic daily transactions, gross margin, rent, payroll and royalty — then see what is left.

    If the unit economics do not work for one outlet, they will not work for five. Scale multiplies a loss as readily as a profit.

  6. Review the agreement with a lawyer

    India has no franchise-specific statute, so the franchise agreement governs nearly everything. Use a lawyer with franchising experience, not just a general commercial lawyer, and have them look hard at:

    • Royalty and advertising fund percentages, and what they are charged on
    • Term and renewal — is renewal a right, or the franchisor's discretion?
    • Territory — how it is defined, whether it is exclusive, and who owns delivery orders placed from inside it
    • Transfer and termination — your exit, which deserves the same scrutiny as your entry

  7. Secure a site and get it approved

    Identify premises and obtain the franchisor's written site approval before signing any lease. Brands assess catchment, visibility, frontage and proximity to existing outlets.

    Never sign a lease before written site approval. It is among the most expensive mistakes in franchising: you are left holding a multi-year rental liability on premises the brand will not let you use.

  8. Complete licensing, fit-out and training

    Registrations vary by activity and state, and commonly include GST registration, a municipal trade licence, and shop and establishment registration. Food businesses additionally require an FSSAI licence. Requirements and thresholds change, so confirm what applies to you with a chartered accountant.

    Run fit-out in parallel with licensing where possible — sequencing them end to end is what stretches a three-month timeline into six.

  9. Soft launch, then open properly

    A soft launch at limited capacity lets you test systems and train staff under real conditions. You give up a little early revenue in exchange for not spending your opening weeks correcting first impressions — which matters most in food service, where early reviews are difficult to undo.

What tends to go wrong

Across first-time franchisees, the same few mistakes recur:

  • Underestimating working capital. The most common avoidable failure. Budget several months of full operating costs beyond setup; outlets rarely reach break-even immediately.
  • Treating the franchise fee as the cost. It is the entry ticket, not the price. Fit-out, deposits, equipment and working capital usually dwarf it.
  • Ignoring the royalty over a full term. A percentage of gross sales across ten years almost always exceeds the up-front fee, and is normally payable whether or not you are profitable.
  • Choosing the brand you like rather than the one that fits. Personal enthusiasm for a product is not a substitute for catchment fit and workable unit economics.
  • Signing a lease before site approval. See above. It happens more often than it should.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to buy and open a franchise in India?

Typically three to six months from first enquiry to opening. Lean formats — kiosks, coaching, home-based services — can move in six to ten weeks once a site is settled. Full-format restaurants and larger retail take longer, mainly because of fit-out and licensing.

Do I need prior business experience?

No. Franchisors provide training and systems precisely so operators without sector experience can run the business. What matters more is available capital, willingness to follow the brand's systems, and whether you will be hands-on or appoint a manager.

What should I check before signing?

Royalty and advertising fund percentages, the term and whether renewal is a right or discretionary, exactly how territory is defined and whether it is exclusive, transfer terms governing your exit, and the termination clause. With no franchise-specific legislation in India, the agreement governs almost everything.

Can I negotiate franchise terms?

Sometimes. Established brands rarely move on royalty rates, since consistency across the network matters to them. Territory boundaries, development schedules, payment timing and the opening support package are more often negotiable — particularly on larger commitments like area developer agreements.
