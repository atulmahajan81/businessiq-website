Franchise vs Your Own Business

Two legitimate routes into business ownership in India, with genuinely different trade-offs. Here is how they compare — including when franchising is the wrong answer.

Home › Learn › Franchise vs Own Business

Where we stand: BusinessIQ is a franchise consultancy, so we have a commercial interest in one of these answers. We have tried to write this straight anyway, including the cases where starting independently is clearly better. Judge the reasoning rather than the source.

The choice is usually framed as safety versus freedom. That is roughly right, but too blunt to decide on. What actually separates the two routes is which problems have already been solved for you — and what you give up in exchange for that.

What a franchise actually buys you

Not safety — compression. A franchisor has already made the expensive mistakes: menu engineering, supplier selection, store layout, staffing ratios, what to do when a shift goes wrong. The operating system is the real product.

That matters most if you are entering a sector you do not know. An independent café owner learns wastage control by throwing stock away for a year. A franchisee is handed the par levels on day one.

What it costs you

Control, and a share of revenue in perpetuity.

The royalty is the part people underweight. A percentage of gross sales, usually payable whether or not you make a profit, across a five- or ten-year term. Over a full term it generally exceeds the up-front fee, often by a wide margin. That is the genuine price of the system — not the entry fee.

Control is the other cost, and it frustrates people more than they expect. You generally cannot change the menu, discount freely, switch to a cheaper supplier, or adjust the branding. If your instinct on reading that was "but I would want to do those things", take the instinct seriously.

A reframe worth applying: a franchise is closer to buying a job with a system attached than to buying an asset you fully control. For many investors that is exactly right. For others it is the wrong purchase entirely — and the agreement is a poor place to discover which you are.

Which fits you?

A franchise likely suits you if…

  • You are entering a sector you have not worked in
  • You have capital but limited time to build systems
  • You would rather follow a proven method than invent one
  • Brand recognition materially helps in your market
  • You want a defined path rather than open-ended problem-solving
  • You plan to add outlets using the same playbook

Your own business likely suits you if…

  • You have real experience in the sector
  • Capital is tight and you need to start small
  • You have a genuinely differentiated idea
  • Control over product and pricing matters to you
  • You want uncapped upside and a clean exit
  • Paying a share of revenue indefinitely would grate

The honest middle ground

These are not the only two options, and the framing hides a few others worth considering.

  • A low-cost franchise first. A service or coaching model from around ₹5 Lakhs buys you the experience of operating within a system without committing a crore.
  • A conversion franchise. If you already run an independent business, some brands let you convert — keeping your premises and team while adopting their systems.
  • Independent first, franchise later. Build something that works, then license it. The most valuable position in franchising is being the franchisor.

Frequently asked questions

Is a franchise safer than starting your own business?

It removes some categories of risk, not risk itself. You inherit a tested model, brand recognition and training, which strips out much of the trial and error. You still carry full exposure to location, local competition, your own execution and the franchisor's performance. A weak franchise in a poor location fails like anything else.

Is a franchise more expensive?

Usually higher up front (franchise fee plus brand-spec fit-out) and higher ongoing (royalty and marketing fund). An independent business can start leaner. The counter-argument is time — a franchise typically reaches trading condition faster and with fewer costly mistakes, which carries its own financial value.

When is starting your own business better?

When you have real sector experience, a differentiated idea, limited capital, or a strong need for control over product and pricing. If you expect to change the menu, the pricing or the branding, franchising will frustrate you — those decisions are contractually not yours.

Can you sell a franchise business?

Usually, but subject to the transfer clause. Franchisors generally approve the buyer and often charge a transfer fee; some hold a right of first refusal. An independent business can be sold to anyone. Read the transfer terms before signing — they govern your exit.
